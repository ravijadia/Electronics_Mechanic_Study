# Chapter: Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs)

**Module:** Opto Electronics — Electronic Mechanic Trade
**Study No.:** 1.11.105 – 1.11.108

---

## 1. Introduction

A **Light Emitting Diode (LED)** is a p-n junction semiconductor device that emits light when it is forward biased. Unlike an ordinary silicon or germanium diode, an LED is made from special compound semiconductor materials that convert electrical energy directly into light energy — a process called **electroluminescence**. LEDs are widely used as indicators, display elements, and light sources because they are small, efficient, long-lasting, and low-voltage.

---

## 2. Construction

- LEDs are made from compound semiconductors such as **Gallium Arsenide (GaAs)**, **Gallium Arsenide Phosphide (GaAsP)**, **Gallium Phosphide (GaP)**, or **Gallium Nitride (GaN)** — not plain silicon, because silicon junctions release energy mainly as heat, not light.
- The p-n junction is formed by doping the compound semiconductor to create p-type and n-type regions.
- The chip is mounted in a reflective cup (to direct light forward) and connected by fine wires to the two leads — **anode (+)** and **cathode (−)**.
- The whole assembly is encapsulated in a transparent or tinted epoxy resin dome, which also acts as a lens to focus/spread the emitted light.
- **Lead identification:** the longer lead is the anode; the cathode side of the epoxy body usually has a flat edge.

---

## 3. Working Principle

1. When the LED is **forward biased**, majority charge carriers (electrons from the n-region, holes from the p-region) are pushed across the junction.
2. At the junction, free electrons **recombine** with holes.
3. Each recombination releases energy. In an LED's compound semiconductor, this energy is released as a **photon of light** (in silicon diodes, it is released as heat/lattice vibration instead).
4. The **wavelength (color)** of the emitted light depends on the **energy bandgap** of the semiconductor material used — a larger bandgap produces higher-energy (shorter wavelength/bluer) light.

```
E (photon energy) = h × f = h × c / λ
```
Where h = Planck's constant, f = frequency, c = speed of light, λ = wavelength.

A larger bandgap material emits shorter wavelength (e.g., blue); a smaller bandgap emits longer wavelength (e.g., red/infrared).

---

## 4. Typical Forward Voltage by Color

| LED Color | Material (typical) | Forward Voltage (Vf) |
|---|---|---|
| Infrared | GaAs | 1.2 – 1.6V |
| Red | GaAsP | 1.8 – 2.2V |
| Yellow / Orange | GaAsP | 2.0 – 2.2V |
| Green | GaP / InGaN | 2.0 – 3.5V |
| Blue | InGaN | 3.0 – 3.6V |
| White | InGaN + phosphor | 3.0 – 3.6V |

Typical forward current (If) for standard indicator LEDs is **10–20 mA**.

---

## 5. Worked Example — Series Current-Limiting Resistor

An LED **must never** be connected directly across a supply — its low resistance would let current rise uncontrolled and destroy it. A series resistor limits the current to a safe value.

**Question:** An LED has Vf = 2V and requires If = 20 mA. It is to be operated from a 5V DC supply. Find the value of the series current-limiting resistor.

**Solution:**
```
R = (Vsupply − Vf) / If
R = (5 − 2) / 0.020
R = 3 / 0.020 = 150 Ω
```

**Answer:** A **150 Ω** resistor in series with the LED limits the current to 20 mA.

---

## 6. Related Opto-Electronic Devices

- **Photodiode:** A p-n junction operated in reverse bias; its reverse leakage current increases in proportion to light falling on the junction — used for light sensing.
- **Phototransistor:** A transistor whose base current is generated by light falling on the base-collector junction, giving higher sensitivity (current gain) than a photodiode.
- **Optocoupler (Optoisolator):** An LED and a photodetector (photodiode/phototransistor) sealed together in one package, facing each other. It transfers a signal between two circuits using light, while keeping them **electrically isolated** — commonly used to protect low-voltage control circuits from high-voltage power circuits.

---

## 7. Key Points to Remember

- LEDs are made from compound semiconductors (GaAs, GaAsP, GaN, etc.), not plain silicon, so recombination energy is released as light, not heat
- Emitted color depends on the semiconductor's energy bandgap
- An LED must always be operated with a series current-limiting resistor
- Forward voltage drop varies by color, typically 1.2V (IR) to 3.6V (blue/white)
- LEDs are strictly polarity-sensitive — reverse voltage beyond a few volts can damage them
- Optocouplers use an LED + photodetector pair to transfer signals with electrical isolation
