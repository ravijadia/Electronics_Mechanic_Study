# Chapter: Soldering and Desoldering of Wires

**Module:** Soldering/Desoldering and Various Switches — Electronic Mechanic Trade
**Study No.:** 1.6.44 – 1.6.47

---

## 1. Introduction

**Soldering** is the process of joining two metal surfaces (usually wires or component leads) by melting a filler metal — **solder** — between them, so that when it cools it forms a strong, low-resistance, permanent electrical and mechanical connection. It is one of the most fundamental hand skills for an Electronic Mechanic, used to assemble and repair every kind of circuit.

---

## 2. Tools and Materials

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Soldering iron** | Heated tool that melts solder onto the joint; common wattages 15W–40W for electronics work |
| **Solder wire** | Filler metal alloy (commonly 60/40 or 63/37 Tin-Lead, or lead-free alloys) with a flux core |
| **Flux** | Chemical that removes oxide film from metal surfaces so solder can bond properly; usually built into the core of solder wire |
| **Soldering iron stand** | Holds the hot iron safely between uses |
| **Wire stripper/cutter** | Removes insulation from wire ends before soldering |
| **Desoldering pump (solder sucker)** | Removes melted solder by suction, to undo a joint or clear a hole |
| **Desoldering wick (braid)** | Copper braid that absorbs melted solder by capillary action when heated on a joint |
| **Small pliers/tweezers** | Holds and positions small wires/components during soldering |

---

## 3. Preparing the Joint

1. **Strip** the wire insulation to expose about 10–15 mm of bare wire (enough to make contact but not so much that it will short against neighboring parts).
2. **Twist** stranded wire strands together so loose strands don't stick out.
3. **Clean** the wire and the surface to be soldered — oxidation, dirt, or old solder residue prevents a good bond. A slightly dull/dirty joint should be lightly cleaned before soldering.
4. **Tin the iron tip** — apply a small amount of fresh solder to a clean, hot iron tip so heat transfers efficiently (this also protects the tip from oxidizing).

---

## 4. Soldering Procedure (Wire-to-Wire / Wire-to-Terminal)

1. Switch on the soldering iron and allow it to reach full temperature (usually 2–3 minutes).
2. Position the wires to be joined so they make firm mechanical contact (e.g. hook or twist them together, or hold them against the terminal).
3. Touch the iron tip to the joint (not the solder) for 1–2 seconds to heat the wire/terminal — heat must reach the joint, not just the surface.
4. Feed the solder wire into the joint (not onto the iron tip) — the heat from the joint should melt it, letting it flow evenly around and into the connection.
5. Remove the solder first, then the iron, once enough solder has flowed to fully coat and fill the joint.
6. Hold the joint still without moving it for a few seconds while it cools and solidifies.
7. Inspect the joint — see Section 6 below.

---

## 5. Desoldering Procedure

Used to remove an existing solder joint, e.g. to replace a component or correct a mistake.

**Using a Desoldering Pump:**

1. Heat the existing solder joint with the iron until it becomes fully molten.
2. Position the desoldering pump nozzle close to the melted solder.
3. Trigger the pump to suck the molten solder away, clearing the joint/hole.
4. Repeat if any solder remains, then gently remove the freed wire/component.

**Using Desoldering Wick (Braid):**

1. Place the braid directly on top of the solder joint.
2. Press the hot iron tip onto the braid over the joint.
3. As the solder melts, it is drawn up into the braid by capillary action.
4. Move to a clean section of braid and repeat if the joint isn't fully cleared, then cut off the used, solder-soaked portion of braid.

---

## 6. Inspecting a Good Solder Joint

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Shiny, smooth, cone-shaped joint** | Good joint — proper heat and solder flow |
| **Dull, grainy, or cracked surface** | "Cold solder joint" — insufficient heat during soldering; unreliable connection, should be reheated and redone |
| **Blob/ball-shaped joint with poor wetting** | Too little heat, or joint was moved before solder solidified |
| **Solder that doesn't stick, beads up** | Surface wasn't clean, or no/insufficient flux |
| **Bridging between adjacent joints** | Excess solder or joints spaced too close — remove bridge with desoldering wick |

---

## 7. Safety Precautions

- Always keep the soldering iron in its stand when not actively soldering — never lay it down loose.
- Solder in a well-ventilated area — rosin flux fumes should not be inhaled directly.
- Never touch the iron tip or freshly soldered joints — they stay hot enough to burn for some time after use.
- Switch off and unplug the iron when finished; allow it to cool fully before storing.
- Keep flammable materials away from the hot iron and its stand.

---

## 8. Key Points to Remember

- Soldering forms a permanent mechanical and electrical joint by melting filler solder between clean metal surfaces
- Heat must be applied to the joint itself, and solder fed into the joint, not the iron tip
- A cold solder joint (dull/cracked) is a common defect caused by insufficient heat — must be redone
- Desoldering pumps use suction; desoldering wick uses capillary action to remove solder
- Flux is essential to remove oxide and allow the solder to properly "wet" and bond to the metal
